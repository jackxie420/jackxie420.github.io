Overview

Our EMNLP 2026 study found that pitch and energy partly carry across translation between English and three European languages. This paper asks whether that holds when languages differ in lexical tone and word order.

We pair English with Mandarin Chinese, Thai, and Vietnamese (tonal), and with Japanese and Korean (very different word order), and compare the tonal languages directly. Correspondence is lower than for European languages. Removing tone-related pitch narrows the gap but does not close it, and greater word reordering goes with weaker local correspondence. The EMNLP results serve as the published European reference throughout this page.

Does the adjustment really remove tone?

A tone classifier gets worse at guessing the tone after adjustment, though it stays above chance. Real tone labels also beat shuffled labels, so the gains come from tone information.

Finding 3

Word order shapes what we can measure

Japanese and Korean reorder content the most and align the least. Keeping each recording's own order (temporal pairing) raises their correlations, but most of that gain disappears once the shared sentence-level pitch decline is removed.

Takeaway

Lexical tone contributes to cross-lingual pitch differences but does not explain them alone. Local prosodic correspondence weakens between languages with very different sentence structure, even when broad sentence-level pitch patterns are shared. Expressive speech translation should model both.
